SUUM

I came for a birthday and left already planning the next visit. I'll be back for the wagyu.

SUUM is a 16-seat room serving a Korean take on omakase. The chef decides, you surrender. It's dark and moody in here, but the lighting over each plate is so well judged that every course looks styled for the camera. The Instagram crowd will be very happy. So was I.

At the helm is chef-owner Andy Choi, and his résumé reads like a pilgrimage. He has cooked at three-Michelin-starred Disfrutar in Barcelona and at The Fat Duck in London, ran his own Seoul restaurant, Andy Pandy, and most recently worked at the Tassis Group's Longwang. SUUM is his first Australian venture. It feels personal: Korean heritage seen through a contemporary fine dining lens, with the heart left firmly intact.

I brought my wife, Sajanie, here for her birthday. Good call.

We went with the SUUM signature pairing: three Korean traditional drinks and three wines. Leave the car at home. Some of the Korean pours on offer here mean business, and you'll want to say yes to all of them.

A round of small bites opened proceedings, a quiet handshake from the kitchen. Then a maesaengi chilled broth. Maesaengi is a fine, silky seaweed from Korea's southern coast, prized as a winter delicacy, and serving it chilled is a nice contemporary twist.

The minari cold noodles were the first dish that made me sit up. They came with abalone liver, and the sauce carried a light bitterness that sounds like a warning on paper. In the bowl it was exactly what the noodles needed, cutting through and keeping every mouthful interesting.

Next came the soy-marinated crab, ganjang gejang. I added the sea urchin roe and ate the two together. No regrets. The urchin brought that sweet, briny richness, the crab brought the depth of soy, and together they were very hard to stop eating. This was Sajanie's favourite dish of the night. We were told that in Korea it's served in the actual crab shell and mixed through rice, a humble, hands-on dish. You can taste that comfort sitting just beneath all the polish.

Samgyetang followed. Back home it's a whole young chicken stuffed with sticky rice, ginseng, jujube and garlic, eaten on the hottest days of summer on the logic of fighting heat with heat. Here it arrives as a refined course, but the idea of a restorative bowl stays front and centre.

Then the hansang, the main course served as a spread. I went for the gochujang duck breast and Sajanie went for the MB9+ wagyu striploin. The duck was fantastic. The gochujang was epic with it, and on any other night I would be writing a whole paragraph about it.

Then I tried the wagyu. O.M.G. Salty, tender, juicy. If you're weighing up the $29 supplement, stop weighing. Pay it.

Alongside, the kitchen sent out a bowl of seaweed and beef soup, miyeok-guk, for the birthday girl. It's the soup Koreans traditionally eat on their birthday, and the same one given to new mothers to help them recover after giving birth. In Sajanie's words, it was wholesome. It's a small gesture, but it told us a lot about how this place thinks.

Dessert was hotteok, the sweet Korean pancake, filled with pumpkin and sunflower seeds. A gentle, nutty finish to a meal that had been anything but gentle on the senses.

We finished with dagwa, some sweets. It's how a Korean host sends you off, and it's a gracious way to close a meal like this.

The staff were genuinely kind and attentive, and made us feel at home from the moment we found the door. They shared the stories behind the dishes, from the crab to the birthday soup, and that made the whole meal feel personal. Food and service together, this was one of my better experiences of 2026.

SUUM is the rare fine dining room that feels both precise and warm. It's clever without being cold, and Korean to its core. I came for a birthday and left already planning the next visit. I'll be back for the wagyu.
